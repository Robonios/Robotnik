# Robotnik Writing Standard

## Front note

This is the writing standard for Robotnik entity profiles. It is drawn from the NVIDIA editorial, the first section written to the deeper template, which passed through six editorial drafts and their edit notes (preceded by an initial sketch) before it reached the final text. The standard governs all profile sections and all 196 profiles that follow the NVIDIA reference.

The document has two parts. **Section 1, The Standard, is the operative part**: the 29 rules to apply, grouped into argumentation (10), prose mechanics (15), and register (4). **Section 2, The Evidence, is the rationale**: the drafts in order, each followed by the notes that moved it to the next. Read Section 1 to write. Read Section 2 to see why each rule is there.

Every rule in Section 1 was paid for by an edit in Section 2. Where a rule looks fussy, the draft that ignored it shows the cost.

## Section 1 — The Standard

### Argumentation (10 rules)

1. **State the actual debate.** Open from the real, documented contest around the subject, not a framing invented for the piece. Say which parts of that debate the profile can answer and which it cannot.
2. **Source every figure, or cut it.** A number that cannot be traced to a filing, an earnings report, or the research sweep does not belong in the text. If it cannot be sourced, remove it or replace it with one that can. A load-bearing statistic no one can cite is the failure this standard exists to prevent.
3. **Explain, do not assert.** Show what a claim means in plain terms. Do not state a conclusion and move on; give the reader the step that earns it.
4. **Define terms at first use.** Every specialist word (for example hyperscaler, high-bandwidth memory, CoWoS, CUDA) gets a plain-English gloss the first time it appears.
5. **Name named things.** Companies, people and figures by name. "SK Hynix", not "one of the three". "Colette Kress, its finance chief", not "a firm". Named entities also give future internal links somewhere to point.
6. **One claim per sentence.** Each sentence carries one step of the argument. Break semicoloned chains into separate sentences.
7. **Answer every question you raise.** Do not raise a question and leave it. If the piece asks what would settle the debate, it answers.
8. **End with the house take.** Close on Robotnik's reading: a position the reader can hold, not a summary that dangles.
9. **Say each thing once.** If a sentence restates a point already made, cut it or make it carry something new.
10. **Write the teaser last.** It states what the body argues, not what an earlier draft argued.

### Prose mechanics (15 rules)

Orwell's six rules, verbatim:

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

The house rules that sit alongside them:

7. **Short sentences.** Prefer the short sentence to the long one. A five-clause sentence is a rewrite waiting to happen.
8. **One idea per paragraph.**
9. **Active voice.** "Three buyers took it", not "it was taken by three buyers".
10. **Numerals for percentages and money.** 90%, not ninety per cent. $4bn, not four billion dollars.
11. **British English throughout.**
12. **"Towards", not "toward".**
13. **"Whilst", not "while".**
14. **No em-dashes.**
15. **Colons sparingly.** Do not use a colon where a full stop and a new sentence would serve.

### Register (4 rules)

1. **Retail-investor legibility.** Write for a non-expert investor. No unexplained jargon, no compression that assumes prior knowledge.
2. **Impersonal.** No first person.
3. **No bylines.**
4. **Zinsser.** Strip the clutter. Every word must do work the reader needs; if it does not, cut it.

### Draft 2

**Editorial**
The debate about NVIDIA is whether a company this dominant can stay dominant, and whether demand this concentrated can last. Almost everyone agrees NVIDIA leads today. The disagreement is about what happens next, and it runs along three fault lines.
The first is whether NVIDIA's dominance is durable or already eroding. Its share of the AI chip market has fallen from roughly ninety per cent in 2023 to the low eighties by 2026. On its face that looks like decline. But the market itself is growing so fast that NVIDIA's revenue keeps rising even as its slice shrinks: a smaller share of a much larger pie is still more pie. The threat worth watching is not a rival chipmaker but NVIDIA's own biggest customers. Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Meta are each designing their own AI chips to reduce what they spend with NVIDIA. The customer and the competitor are becoming the same company.
The second fault line is whether the demand can hold. The AI build-out is funded by a small number of buyers spending enormous sums, and those same buyers are the ones building alternatives. So the demand that powers NVIDIA's growth and the competition that threatens it come from one place. If those buyers slow their spending, or shift it to their own chips, both problems arrive together.
The third is the reading specific to Robotnik, and it concerns where NVIDIA is strong versus where it is exposed. NVIDIA is strong with its customers: its software and networking lock them in, so it sets the terms of sale. It is weak with its suppliers: it makes none of its own chips, depending entirely on TSMC to fabricate them, on TSMC's packaging capacity to assemble them, and on a few memory makers to feed them. It is also exposed to politics, because that entire supply chain runs through Taiwan and Korea and sits inside the US-China export fight. The point is that the real near-term risks to NVIDIA are physical and political, not competitive. The thing most likely to constrain it is not losing a customer but failing to secure enough manufacturing, or a policy shock to the supply chain.
None of this resolves the debate, and it is not meant to. NVIDIA's position is genuinely strong and genuinely contested at the same time. What the three fault lines show is where to look: not at market share, which flatters, but at manufacturing capacity and export policy, which bind.

Will the demand last? Its leadership describes a shift from near-total dependence on the hyperscalers towards a rough half-and-half split with governments and enterprises. Gulf, European and Asian states are funding national AI programmes. Firms such as CoreWeave rent NVIDIA systems to companies that cannot build their own. The product is the same in every case, and that does not matter much. What matters is that more hands are paying for it, because a broader customer base means no single buyer's retreat is fatal. The caveat is that all these buyers are making the same bet, that AI compute pays for itself, so the diversification protects against one customer pulling back, not against the bet itself failing. The only revenue resting on a different bet is robotics and vehicles, and at $2.3 billion of $215.9 billion in fiscal 2026, it is 1% of the total. NVIDIA, for its part, is investing as if the demand holds. It has signed long-term memory contracts and paid suppliers years in advance. Colette Kress, its finance chief, returned the company to the bond market in 2026, raising $25 billion, its first debt since 2021, to help fund those commitments.
Where does NVIDIA sit in the chain of dependence? Robotnik maps the frontier stack as a chain: who relies on whom, and who could be replaced. Seen through that map, NVIDIA splits in two.
Facing its customers, NVIDIA sets the terms, including for the four building alternatives. They pay while they build because leaving costs years. Their AI software runs on CUDA, NVIDIA's programming platform. Their data centres are wired with NVIDIA's networking. Both would have to be rebuilt. Their deeper moat is the product roadmap. NVIDIA publishes its chips two to three years ahead, Rubin this year, Rubin Ultra in 2027, Feynman in 2028, and the industry invests against the plan. SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron have begun volume production of next-generation memory for Vera Rubin, a chip NVIDIA has not yet shipped, with SK Hynix estimated to hold 60 to 70% of that work. A challenger cannot simply design a better processor. It must persuade the same suppliers to retool around its roadmap instead, and their most profitable lines are already committed to NVIDIA's. That synchronisation is a moat measured in years.
Facing its suppliers, NVIDIA takes the terms, because it has nowhere else to go. It designs chips but makes none. TSMC both fabricates its processors, at a precision no other manufacturer matches today, and packages them, joining processor to memory in a process called CoWoS whose capacity is committed long in advance. SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron have sold their memory production years ahead, so more cannot be had quickly at any price. Further up the chain sit Ibiden, the Japanese firm that leads in the advanced substrates the largest chips are built on, and ASML, whose lithography is unique to it. Does NVIDIA's size win any of this power back? Some. It is among TSMC's largest customers, and TSMC would feel the loss of that volume. But weight is not leverage when there is no alternative. TSMC could reallocate its capacity to Apple or AMD; NVIDIA cannot move its orders anywhere at the leading edge. The dependence runs both ways, and it does not run equally. Jensen Huang appears to have drawn the same conclusion, because he is paying to secure the chain. NVIDIA put $4 billion into its optical suppliers this year, signed long-term memory contracts, and prepaid capacity. That is the investment of a company whose ceiling is set by what it can build.
Politics runs through all of it. The chain above sits in Taiwan, Korea, Japan and the Netherlands. The raw materials beneath it, the gallium in NVIDIA's power electronics, the indium in its optics, the rare earths in everything that moves, need Chinese export licences, and the truce covering those materials lapses in November. The precedent worth holding onto is the one that did no damage. When Washington and then Beijing closed China's market to NVIDIA's chips, growth went around the loss. The export risk that binds NVIDIA is not whether its chips can flow into China. It is whether the materials keep flowing out. The full chronology sits in this profile's policy layer; the geography of the chain sits in its geography layer.
What would settle the debate? Four things. The capital-spending guidance of the hyperscalers, which funds everything. The share of their chip budgets going to their own designs, which measures substitution. Whether NVIDIA's packaging and memory allocations turn into shipped systems, which is the supply limit made visible. And whether the materials truce holds past November, which is the political limit made visible.
Robotnik's reading is that the loud contest is the slow one. Customers replacing NVIDIA is a matter of years. A licence decision or a packaging shortfall is a matter of months. NVIDIA is harder to attack than the competition story suggests, and easier to stop than its results suggest, because its weaknesses sit where an income statement does not show them: in the suppliers above it and the politics around them. Watch this company from upstream.
