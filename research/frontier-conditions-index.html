The Frontier Conditions Index: Reading the Direction of the Stack

A monthly diffusion index on a scale from -100 to +100 that reads whether the frontier stack's condition is improving or deteriorating, and how broadly, rather than what the stack is worth.

The rest of the index family reads the stack's level and its exposure. The price indices say what it is worth; the bottleneck-weighted index says where it is most exposed to a supply shock. The Frontier Conditions Index reads something others do not: the direction of the stack's condition, and how broad-based that direction is. It answers whether things are getting better or worse across the stack as a whole, on a diffusion scale from -100 to +100, where zero is neutral, a positive reading means conditions are broadly improving, and a negative one that they are broadly deteriorating. It is a conditions gauge, closer in spirit to a purchasing-managers' index than to a price.

Why breadth, and not level

A price index can rise on the strength of a single name. One mega-cap climbing carries the whole index with it, even while most of the stack is flat or falling, so a rising level does not tell you the improvement is broad. The conditions index asks the different question: how many parts of the stack are improving at once. That is breadth, and it is participation rather than price.

The distinction matters because a single giant carries its own weather. A dominant chipmaker can rise or fall on forces particular to it, forces that say little about the condition of robotics, space, or materials. Measured by breadth, that idiosyncratic move counts for one constituent among many rather than moving the whole gauge, so the index registers a genuine change in the stack's condition rather than the fortunes of its largest member.

The four components

The index is the equal-weighted average of four component scores, each mapped onto the same -100 to +100 scale.

Equity breadth is the share of the roughly 197 public constituents trading above their own twelve-month moving average. Half of them above the line reads as zero, all of them as +100, none as -100. It is company-level participation: how much of the stack, name by name, is in an uptrend.

Sector breadth is how many of the four sector indices, semiconductors, robotics, space, and materials, sit above their own twelve-month average. Two of four reads as zero, four as +100, none as -100. These use the plain cap-weighted sector indices rather than the bottleneck-adjusted ones, so that this gauge stays independent of the supply-risk lens and measures dispersion across the stack, not exposure within it.

Private-capital direction is the trend in the private-capital index, the month-on-month movement in private-market fundraising and conviction.

Commodity input stress is the direction of the commodities index, inverted. Rising input costs are a deteriorating condition for the stack that consumes them, so a rising commodities reading pulls this component down.

The two trend components, and the one calibration

The two breadth components map onto the scale by construction, straight from the share above a moving average. The two trend components do not: a month's movement in private capital or in commodities is an unbounded percentage, not a share, so it needs a scaling constant to sit on the same -100 to +100 range as the others.

That constant is set so that a typical monthly move lands in the middle of the scale rather than pinning its ends, calibrated against the historical monthly volatility of each series. It is the one deliberate calibration in the index, a construction parameter rather than a claim about the world, and it is chosen so that the trend components speak at the same volume as the breadth components rather than drowning them or being drowned.

The equity tilt

Two of the four components, equity breadth and sector breadth, are drawn from the same public-equity prices, so equity conditions carry about half of the signal. This is deliberate: public equities are the core of the stack, and the two are not duplicates. Equity breadth measures participation name by name; sector breadth measures dispersion across sectors, whether the strength is broad or concentrated in one corner. A leaner three-pillar version, equity, private capital, and commodities at a third each, was considered and set aside in favour of keeping the four and accepting the tilt, because dispersion across sectors is worth reading in its own right.

Cadence, and how to read it

The index is monthly. Its slowest input, private capital, moves monthly, and a conditions gauge read weekly would report noise as signal. A purchasing-managers' index is the right analogy for the pace as well as the shape.

Read the sign first and the magnitude second. The sign gives the direction, whether the stack's condition is on balance improving or deteriorating; the magnitude gives the breadth, how widely shared that direction is. A reading near zero is a stack pulling in different directions at once; a reading near either extreme is one moving together.

How it fits the family

The family now reads the stack three ways. The price indices give its level, the bottleneck-weighted index its supply exposure, and this index the direction and breadth of its condition. The three answer different questions and are meant to be read together: what the stack is worth, where it is fragile, and which way it is moving. This index is also the one that draws on the others, since its components are the public-equities and sector indices, the private-capital index, and the commodities index, each read for direction rather than level.

What to bear in mind

Breadth is bought at the cost of magnitude. By counting how many things are improving rather than by how much, the index deliberately mutes a narrow but violent move, which is the point when that move is one mega-cap's own story, but a limit when the concentrated move is the signal. The gauge tells you how broad a shift is, not how large.

The equity tilt is real. About half the signal comes from public-equity prices, so a month in which equities and the private and commodity components disagree will lean towards the equity reading.

The trend components rest on a calibration. The scaling constant that fits private-capital and commodity movement onto the scale is a chosen parameter, reviewed against the series' volatility rather than derived from a law, and a different choice would shift those two components' contribution.

And it is monthly and young. It moves slowly by design, and its published series is short, so its readings are best understood as they accumulate into a track rather than one at a time.

Frequently asked questions

What does the Frontier Conditions Index measure?

The direction and breadth of the frontier stack's condition, on a diffusion scale from -100 to +100. Zero is neutral, a positive reading means conditions are broadly improving, and a negative one that they are broadly deteriorating. It reads whether the stack is getting better or worse across the board, not what it is worth.

How is it different from the price indices?

The price indices measure the stack's level, which can rise on a single large name even while most of the stack is flat. This index measures how many parts of the stack are improving at once, which is breadth rather than price, so a single giant's move counts for one constituent rather than moving the whole gauge.

What are the four components?

Equity breadth (the share of the public constituents above their twelve-month average), sector breadth (how many of the four sector indices are above theirs), private-capital direction (the trend in the private-capital index), and commodity input stress (the commodities index direction, inverted, since rising input costs worsen conditions). They are equal-weighted.

Why is the commodities component inverted?

Because commodities are an input cost to the stack, not an output of it. When input prices rise, conditions for the companies that consume those inputs worsen, so a rising commodities reading lowers this component rather than raising it.

Why is it monthly rather than weekly?

Because it is a conditions gauge, and its slowest input, private capital, moves monthly. Read weekly it would report noise as signal. A monthly cadence matches the pace of what it measures, in the spirit of a purchasing-managers' index.

Does equity carry too much weight?

Two of the four components are drawn from equity prices, so equity conditions are about half the signal. This is deliberate, since public equities are the core of the stack, and the two equity components are not duplicates: one measures participation name by name, the other dispersion across sectors.
